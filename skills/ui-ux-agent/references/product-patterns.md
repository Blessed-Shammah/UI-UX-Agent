# Product patterns: choose by the user's job

These are starting hypotheses, not mandatory page templates. Adapt the information hierarchy to the product, audience, and available evidence.

## SaaS marketing and product landing pages

The buyer needs to understand the offer, see it working, assess fit, and choose a next step. Lead with the specific outcome and a believable product view or demonstration. Organize supporting content around buyer questions: what happens, who it serves, what it costs, and what commitment is required. Use real proof only; omit a logo wall or testimonial section if there is none. Connect calls to action to a working destination. A novel hero cannot rescue an unclear offer.

Choose imagery that demonstrates the actual service. A free marketing template can supply structure, but remove irrelevant sections rather than filling them with invented claims. Keep pricing, billing interval, and included features explicit when provided.

## SaaS applications and operational dashboards

The user needs to decide or act, often repeatedly. Start with the queue, next action, exceptions, or current work; add summary metrics only when they help a decision. Use tables for comparison, lists for prioritization, and detail panels for context. Define search, filters, selection, pagination or loading, and no-results behavior together. Monetary and status data require consistent formatting and semantics. Avoid decorative charts and redundant statistics.

## Commerce and booking

The user needs confidence about suitability, cost, availability, and what happens next. Prioritize product/service information, authentic imagery, selectable options, total cost, and a legible commitment action. Preserve selections when validation fails. Explain stock, booking conflicts, time zones, cancellation, and checkout status where relevant. Do not imply a successful purchase or reservation without the necessary backend result. Use established purchasing conventions; make the brand distinctive through content and art direction.

## Editorial, portfolio, and creative work

The work and its story should carry the identity. Choose a typographic rhythm and image sequence appropriate to reading and browsing. Give projects meaningful context, credits, and descriptive navigation. Make visual experiments compatible with readable text and discoverable contact/navigation. Avoid motion that delays access to the work or makes the site difficult to scroll. Use only rights-cleared project imagery.

## Mobile capture and consumer tasks

Prioritize the frequent task and one-handed use. Reduce repeated input, preserve drafts, make permission denial recoverable, and distinguish local completion from remote synchronization. Use platform navigation and familiar controls where useful. Match feedback to the user's real environment: bright light, interruption, unreliable network, or short sessions. Decorative onboarding should not delay a task the person already understands.

## Desktop professional tools

Prioritize continuity, keyboard access, working context, and adjustable density. Lists, tables, split views, and inspectors often serve better than a succession of full-page cards. Keep selection visible and command scope explicit. Handle long operations, unsaved changes, file errors, and window resizing. Avoid a visually impressive shell around simulated controls when the task requires real integration.
